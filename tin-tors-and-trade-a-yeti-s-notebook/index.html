Why is tin so tricky?

Dear notebook, I spent a whole afternoon wondering why anyone bothered with tin at all. Then I read the numbers and my fur stood on end. Tin is incredibly rare in the Earth's crust, about 2 parts per million. Copper, by comparison, sits around 70 ppm.

Yeti fact: Ancient smiths couldn't just dig tin up anywhere. They had to find the right rocks, in the right places, and then carry the metal a very long way.

Bronze is copper mixed with tin, so no tin, no bronze. No bronze, no swords, no fancy brooches. Everything hangs on that little bit of tin.

Cornish mines and the tin tors

Cornwall and west Devon are among the few places in western Europe where tin ore was worked in quantity. The granite uplands, or tors, were rich in cassiterite, a heavy brown-black tin mineral that sinks in streams.

Streaming and stone

Early miners did not always dig deep shafts. They worked the streams, washing gravel in wooden troughs so the heavy tin ore settled out. Later came pits, adits and engine houses, with steam pumps keeping the water out.

Hot tip from a cold yeti: the engine houses still stand on the hillsides, and they look wonderful against a grey sky.

The Bronze Age tin trade

Tin had to travel. Cornish and Devon tin probably moved along coastal routes and rivers, perhaps reaching Brittany and beyond, and it may have reached the Mediterranean through trade networks that stretched right across Europe.

  • Stream tin: gathered from riverbeds, washed and bagged.
  • Overland and coastal carriage: ingots and lumps passed from hand to hand.
  • Trading ships: boats carried metal to far-off harbours, where it was traded for other goods.
  • The smith's forge: tin met copper and became bronze, and the bronze became tools, weapons and jewellery.

I like to imagine a ship full of tin ingots, creaking along a grey coast, with a little furry sailor counting the ingots twice.

Smiths, moulds and making things twice

Bronze Age smiths were clever. Many used lost-wax casting, also called cire perdue. You make a model in wax, cover it in clay, melt the wax out, and pour in hot metal. The clay mould then has to be broken to free the finished piece, so each one was truly one of a kind.

Why I love this: It is a bit like copying a sculpture: you copy the original, then cast the copy in metal. It is slow, careful work, and I respect it enormously.
