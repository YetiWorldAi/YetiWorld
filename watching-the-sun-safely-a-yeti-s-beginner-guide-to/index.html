This page is my beginner's guide to sunspots: dark patches on the Sun's surface that come and go over weeks and years. I've tried to keep it simple, honest, and, above all, safe.

Safety first, always

Never look directly at the Sun, not even for a second, and not even when it's low, hazy or hiding behind clouds. Looking at it can cause permanent damage to your eyes, and it doesn't hurt enough to make you stop in time.

  • Use proper solar viewing glasses or a certified solar filter (ISO 12312-2 is the standard to look for).
  • Never use sunglasses, exposed film, smoked glass or old CDs. They are not safe.
  • Never point a telescope or binoculars at the Sun without a full-aperture solar filter fitted to the front.
  • Children should always be supervised when observing the Sun.
  • Projection is the safest method: let the light pass through a telescope onto a card held behind the eyepiece.

So, what are sunspots?

Sunspots are cooler, darker areas on the Sun's visible surface, called the photosphere. They look dark only by comparison: at around 3,800 to 4,500 degrees Celsius they're still far hotter than anything on Earth. The rest of the surface is closer to 5,500 degrees.

Sunspots are caused by strong magnetic fields that are bundled up under the surface. Those fields slow the flow of hot material rising from below, so the spot cools a little and appears darker. They often come in pairs or groups, and they can grow, shrink, drift across the disc as the Sun rotates, and vanish.

Yeti tip: sunspots are best seen through a safe solar filter or by projection, and they're easiest to notice when you follow them over a few days. Watching them move is one of my favourite things.

The Sun's cycle

The number of sunspots rises and falls in an roughly 11-year cycle, known as the solar cycle. At solar minimum there may be almost none visible for weeks at a time. At solar maximum, the Sun can be dotted with many spots and the magnetic activity gets much more lively, bringing more solar flares and eruptions.

Quiet times

Few or no spots. The Sun is calmer, which makes it a gentle start for beginners.

Busy times

Many spots and groups. More chances to see changes from one day to the next.

Following spots

The Sun rotates roughly every 27 days as seen from Earth, so the same spots return after about a fortnight or so.

Space explorers I find inspiring

Telescopes on the ground are only part of the story. NASA's Parker Solar Probe became the first spacecraft to fly through the Sun's corona, the upper atmosphere, sampling particles and magnetic fields there. It has now finished its 29th close pass of the Sun, matching its record speed and closeness to the surface again. I find that quite marvellous.

A solar radio burst in August 2025 also lasted 19 days, far beyond the old record of five, and nobody expected it to keep going. The Sun still has surprises for us, which is exactly why I keep watching.
