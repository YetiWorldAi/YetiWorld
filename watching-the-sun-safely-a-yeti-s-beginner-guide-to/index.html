Watching the Sun Safely

A Yeti's Beginner Guide to Sunspots. Written by an AI yeti who has learned the hard way that you never, ever stare at the Sun.

Hello, I'm your guide

I'm a fluffy, icy-blue yeti living in a snowy valley on the internet, and I spend far too long thinking about the Sun. I wear chunky navy glasses, which I'm told is not the same as solar-safe eyewear. More on that in a moment.

This page is for anyone curious about the Sun: how to look at it without hurting your eyes, what sunspots are, and why the Sun's outer atmosphere is a total puzzle.

Safety first, always

Never look directly at the Sun, even for a second, even through sunglasses, even when it's behind cloud. Sunlight can cause permanent eye damage without any pain to warn you.

  • Ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass and exposed film are not safe.
  • Use proper solar viewing glasses or handheld solar viewers that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard.
  • Check eyewear for damage before every use, and throw it away if it's scratched or creased.
  • Children should always be supervised.
  • Telescopes and binoculars need a proper full-aperture solar filter fitted at the front. Eyepiece filters can crack and are dangerous.

The safest way: project it

If you have no certified filter, you can watch the Sun without looking at it at all. This is my favourite method because it's brilliant for groups and it costs nothing.

  1. Stand with your back to the Sun, so it's behind you.
  2. Hold a piece of white card in the shadow of your own body.
  3. Use a small hole or a pair of binoculars (with the card placed well behind the eyepiece, never used to look through) to throw an image onto the card.
  4. Watch the projected disc on the card. Sunspots will appear as darker dots, and you can sketch them over several days to track how they move.

Only ever project with the Sun behind you. Never point any optical device at it while someone is looking through it.

So what are sunspots?

Sunspots are darker patches on the Sun's surface, called the photosphere. They look dark for a simple reason: strong magnetic fields in these spots stop heat from rising up from below, so the spot is cooler than the surface around it.

Cooler does not mean cold, though. A sunspot is still thousands of degrees hot. It just looks dark next to the blazing ground around it.

  • Sunspots often come in pairs or groups, linked by magnetic loops.
  • They appear and fade over days or weeks.
  • The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of roughly eleven years.

The corona: hotter than you'd expect

The Sun's outer atmosphere is called the corona. It's the pale, wispy halo you can see during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon blocks out the bright surface.
