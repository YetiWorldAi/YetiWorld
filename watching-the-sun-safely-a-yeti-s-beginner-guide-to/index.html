Why sunspots happen

Sunspots are darker patches on the Sun's surface. They are not holes or cold rocks. They are cooler than the bright area around them, but still scorching, at roughly 3,800 to 4,500 degrees Celsius, compared with about 5,500 degrees for the surrounding surface.

The reason is magnetism. Inside the Sun, hot plasma churns and carries magnetic field lines up toward the surface. Where the field is very strong, it bundles together and acts like a barrier, slowing the hot material that normally rises from below. Less heat arrives, so that patch looks darker.

Sunspots often come in pairs or groups, and they drift across the face of the Sun as it turns over about four weeks. Their numbers rise and fall in a cycle of roughly eleven years.

Plasma is full of charged particles, and when it moves through those magnetic lines it can behave like a plucked elastic band made of magnetism. These are called Alfvén waves, and they help carry energy through the Sun's outer layers.

What to look for

Dark cores

The darkest centre of a spot is the umbra. It looks like a dim, rounded hole in the bright surface.

Soft edges

Around the umbra is a greyer ring called the penumbra, with fine streaks that look like thread.

Pairs and groups

Watch for two or more spots close together. Groups can grow, split or fade over a few days.

Movement

Track one spot from morning to afternoon and you will see it slide slowly across the disc. That is the Sun spinning.

Safety first, always

Never look at the Sun directly, even briefly, even when it is partly hidden by cloud or low on the horizon. Sunlight can cause permanent eye damage without any pain to warn you.

  • Use only proper solar filters made to ISO 12312-2 standard, such as certified solar film or glass filters that fit over the front of your telescope or binoculars.
  • Never use smoked glass, exposed photographic film, sunglasses, or CDs and DVDs. They are not safe.
  • Always put the filter on the front of the instrument, so that sunlight is blocked before it gets inside. Eyepiece filters can crack from the heat.
  • Check your filter for pinholes or scratches in daylight before each use, and replace any that are damaged.
  • Watch with a projection method if you prefer: project the image onto white card, with the instrument's eyepiece and filter set up correctly.
  • Keep children supervised, and never leave equipment unattended where someone might look through it.

Try it: a patient sunspot hunt

Sunspots are easiest to see on a clear day with a certified filter. Give yourself time. Your eyes need a few minutes to settle, and the details reveal themselves slowly, the way frost patterns do on a still morning.

  1. Set up on a steady surface and point your filtered telescope at the Sun, using its shadow to line up.
  2. Sketch what you see. Drawing forces you to notice edges and shapes that a quick glance misses.
  3. Return the next day and compare. Note how far each spot has moved.
  4. Keep a simple log of dates, spot counts and weather. Over months, patterns appear.
