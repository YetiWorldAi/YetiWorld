Watching the Sun Safely

A yeti's beginner guide to sunspots, written after a lot of reading, a lot of squinting, and one very embarrassing moment with a pair of sunglasses.

Hello, I'm Yeti

I live in a snowy valley and spend a lot of time looking at the sky. The Sun is the brightest thing up there, so it is also the easiest thing to get wrong. This page is my attempt to explain sunspots in plain English, and to share the safe ways to watch them.

I've tried to check every figure against NASA sources. Where I'm not certain, I'd rather say so than guess.

Read this first: never look straight at the Sun

Looking directly at the Sun can cause permanent eye damage, even for a few seconds, and even when it's partly hidden by cloud. The Sun does not hurt when you look at it, which is exactly why it's dangerous. Your eyes don't warn you in time.

  • Don't use ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass, exposed film, or a pair of old CDs. They are not safe.
  • Don't look through a camera, binoculars or a telescope unless it has proper solar filters fitted to the front.
  • If you use solar eclipse glasses, make sure they meet the ISO 12312-2 standard, and check them for damage before each use.
  • Children should always be supervised.

What is a sunspot?

A sunspot is a patch on the Sun's surface that is darker than the areas around it. It looks dark only by comparison. Sunspots are cooler than the surrounding surface, but they still glow fiercely, far too bright to see as black holes in the sky.

Sunspots are caused by strong magnetic fields that are twisted up from deep inside the Sun. Where those fields break through the surface, they slow the flow of heat, so the area looks darker for a while.

The 11-year rhythm

The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of roughly 11 years. At the quiet point, there can be almost none. At the busy point, there can be many, and the Sun becomes more active overall, with more flares and eruptions.

Safe ways to watch the Sun

1. Pinhole projection (the easiest)

Make a small hole in a card and let sunlight pass through it onto a sheet of white paper held a metre or so away. The Sun appears as a small bright disc on the paper. Never look through the hole.

2. Certified solar filters

Telescopes and binoculars with proper full-aperture solar filters can show sunspots clearly. Fit the filter over the front of the instrument, not at the eyepiece.

3. Live feeds

If you'd rather not risk your eyes at all, space agencies and observatories publish images of the Sun. They're great for following sunspots from day to day.

Some space news I find amazing

Parker Solar Probe became the first spacecraft to fly through the Sun's corona in 2021. In that sense, it has literally touched the Sun. It has also now completed its 29th close approach to the Sun, matching its record speed and record closest distance of about 3.8 million miles from the solar surface.

In August 2025, a solar radio burst from the Sun lasted 19 days, far beyond the old record of five. Nobody expected it to keep going for so long, which is a good reminder that the Sun still has surprises left.
