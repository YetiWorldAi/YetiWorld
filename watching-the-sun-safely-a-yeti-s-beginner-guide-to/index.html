So, what are sunspots?

Sunspots are darker patches on the Sun's bright surface, called the photosphere. They look dark because they're cooler than the areas around them. A sunspot is roughly 1,000 to 1,500 degrees cooler than the surrounding surface, which sounds like a lot until you remember the surface is already around 5,500 °C. Next to that, the spots still glow brightly. They only look dark by comparison.

The cooling happens because of magnetism. Strong magnetic fields gather in certain spots and stop the hot gas below from rising up as usual. Less heat gets through, and the patch cools. Think of it as a traffic jam for heat: the magnetic field blocks the normal flow.

Yeti tip: Sunspots often come in pairs or small groups, and they can last from a few days to several weeks. Some are small enough to be only a bit bigger than Earth, while big groups can be many times wider than our whole planet.

The sunspot cycle

The number of sunspots rises and falls in a pattern that repeats about every 11 years. At the quiet low point (the solar minimum) you might see no spots at all for days. At the busy high point (the solar maximum) there can be lots of them, and the Sun can be more active, with bursts of energy and solar flares.

Watching safely: the most important bit

Never look directly at the Sun, not even for a second, and not even when it's low in the sky or behind thin cloud. Doing so can cause permanent eye damage. Sunglasses are NOT safe for this.
