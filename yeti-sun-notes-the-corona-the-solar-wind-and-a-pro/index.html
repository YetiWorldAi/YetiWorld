The Corona: Why Is the Outside Hotter?

Right, picture this. The surface of the Sun sits at roughly 10,000 degrees Fahrenheit. Sensible enough. Then you look at the corona, the thin, wispy outer atmosphere above it, and it gets to around 2 million degrees Fahrenheit.

That makes no sense on the face of it. You'd expect things to cool as you move away from a heat source, like standing back from a fire. Instead, the outside gets hotter. I have been staring at that fact for a long time, and it still makes my glasses steam up.

Yeti's fact: The corona is hundreds of times hotter than the surface beneath it, which is exactly why scientists call it a curious case.

The Hot Corona Mystery

Solar physicists have been chewing on this one for decades. The heat must be coming from somewhere, and the energy has to travel up from below through magnetic fields and waves. The trouble is working out exactly how it gets delivered.

I like to think of it as a puzzle with the most dramatic clue in the solar system. Every answer people find seems to raise two new questions, which, honestly, is the best sort of puzzle.

The Solar Wind

The corona does more than glow. It is constantly streaming outwards as the solar wind: a flow of charged particles that rushes out through the whole solar system, past the planets and out into the dark beyond.

When you stand in a valley like mine, you can feel a breeze. The solar wind is a breeze too, only it is made of particles moving at enormous speed and it never really stops.

It never stops

The Sun breathes out its wind all the time, so there is always a little push of it flowing past Earth.

It's charged

Electrically charged particles get pushed and bent by magnetic fields, which is why the wind shapes so much around it.

It goes far

The wind travels well beyond the planets, filling the space between them with its gentle stream.

Parker Solar Probe: A Probe That Dares

NASA's Parker Solar Probe became the first spacecraft to fly through the Sun's corona in 2021. That is about as close as anything has ever got to touching a star. I find that thought almost too big to hold, so I keep poking at it.

Each close approach brings it back to the Sun, and on each one it has matched its record closest pass: about 3.8 million miles from the surface. Going back again and again, and getting the same result, takes real nerve and very careful engineering.

Yeti's fact: Parker keeps matching its record closest pass, around 3.8 million miles from the Sun's surface, on each of its close approaches.
