The corona: the Sun's wispy crown

The Sun has a visible surface, but above it is a huge, thin atmosphere called the corona. Normally it's drowned out by the brilliant light of the surface. You can only properly see it during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon blocks the bright disc and pearly streamers fan out into space.

Here's the puzzle that makes scientists scratch their heads: the surface is around 5,500 degrees Celsius, but the corona is over a million degrees. It's hotter the further you get from the heat source, which is the opposite of what you'd expect. Nobody has a complete answer yet, though waves and magnetic energy are the leading suspects.

The solar wind: a stream from the Sun

The corona is so hot that its particles escape the Sun's gravity. They pour outwards as a constant stream of charged particles, mostly protons and electrons, called the solar wind. It moves at hundreds of kilometres per second and fills the whole Solar System, bumping into planets and sculpting the space around them.

Magnetic switchbacks: the field bends back

Close to the Sun, the probe found something unexpected: sudden reversals in the solar wind's magnetic field. The field lines fold back on themselves in zigzags, like a path that doubles back as you walk along it. These are called switchbacks.

Nobody is completely sure how switchbacks form. One idea is that they grow from waves on the Sun's surface, and another is that they're created by reconnection low down in the corona. I'm rooting for the second, because it sounds like a snowball fight between magnetic fields.

Magnetic reconnection: snapping lines

Imagine two magnetic field lines pointing in opposite directions get pushed together. They break and rejoin in a new arrangement, and in the process they release a burst of stored energy. That's magnetic reconnection.

It's behind solar flares and coronal mass ejections, and it likely helps heat the corona and kick the solar wind into motion. The energy has been building up in twisted, tangled magnetic fields for a while, then suddenly snaps free. Honestly, it's the most dramatic bit of tidying up in the entire Solar System.

Strong flares tend to come from regions where the magnetic field is highly twisted, so energy builds up before it's released.
